About the Rulebook Workbench

A one-page orientation for anyone opening the workbench for the first time. No technical background needed.

The problem

Every regulated industry lives under a body of rules that no one designed as a whole. Statutes get amended piecemeal. Regulations layer on top of statutes. Agencies add guidance, reporting instructions, examination manuals, FAQs — and enforcement actions imply expectations that appear nowhere in writing. After a few decades the result is a Legacy Rulebook: thousands of provisions that each made sense when written but that collectively contain duplications, contradictions, dead references, and requirements scattered across so many documents that no one can find them all. Everyone senses the mess; no one can see it whole.

Think of it as a building assembled over a century by different crews, with no master drawing on file. Before renovating, a sensible owner would first draw the building as it actually stands — crooked hallways included.

The method, in four moves

Legacy Rulebook → ① Distill → Derived Blueprint → ②a Refactor / ②b Redesign → Target Blueprint → ③ Align → Consolidated Rulebook   (④ Reform only through this path)
① Distill → the Derived Blueprint. AI reads the entire Legacy Rulebook and produces a faithful, higher-level picture of the operating logic actually in force — what the regime is trying to accomplish, who must do what, and how the pieces interact. Faithful means faithful: where the rulebook contradicts itself, the blueprint records the contradiction rather than quietly fixing it. Every statement traces to the specific statute, regulation, or guidance it came from, and the quote is machine-checked against the source.
②a Refactor / ②b Redesign → the Target Blueprint. This is where human attention concentrates, and it fixes the drawing, not the bricks. Every program declares its mode up front. Refactor is cleanup only: merge duplicates, give each term one definition, retire dead rules — without changing what the law substantively requires. Redesign goes further: it starts from a written statement of what the regime is for, adopted by the people with authority to hold those objectives, and reshapes the blueprint to serve them. Redesign is openly a policy exercise, so it runs under stricter governance, not looser: every change names the objective it serves, and every change is decided by a named person, never by the tool.
③ Align → the Consolidated Rulebook. Redraft the operative materials to match the cleaned blueprint, with a complete crosswalk showing where every old provision went — merged, repealed, or retained, each with reasons. Nothing disappears silently.
④ Reform. Actual reform — repealing old materials and adopting new ones — is not a shortcut around this process; it happens only through it, executed by the authority that owns the rules, with these materials as the working papers.

Two disciplines make it trustworthy

First, the separation between cleanup and policy change is enforced by rule, not good intentions: the mode is declared up front, policy change is permitted only in redesign mode, and even there only when anchored to an adopted objective and decided by a named person. Second, the paper trail: every statement traces to its source, and every human judgment is recorded with who decided, what they decided, and why. Anyone — a colleague today, the public later — can check the work.

What the workbench is not. It is an analytical workbench with drafting support. Everything it produces — blueprints, registers, crosswalks, proposed drafts — is an advisory working paper addressed to an Authority. It never produces operative legal text; promulgation and repeal are the Authority's acts.

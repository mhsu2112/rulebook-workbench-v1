How to Read the Workbench

A plain-English companion for policy readers. No technical background needed. It explains, in order, what each tab is, how to read each document the workbench produces, and what the recurring terms mean. Ten minutes cover to cover; or jump to the tab you're looking at.

The one-paragraph picture

A body of regulation is like a building added onto for a century with no master drawing. This tool does four things, in order. It reads the whole rulebook and draws it as it actually stands (the Derived Blueprint). A person then either cleans up the drawing without changing what the rules require (Refactor) or reshapes it to serve stated goals (Redesign), producing the Target Blueprint. Finally it shows where every original rule ended up (the Crosswalk, under Align). Nothing it produces is law — it produces working papers for the authority that owns the rules. The tabs across the top follow exactly that order, left to right.

The screen, in three parts

Left panel — the list of Programs (each is one consolidation project), the Guides (this document, the About page, and the reviewer guides), and at the bottom your name and a Read only switch that hides all the action buttons so you can look without touching anything.

Middle panel — the working area. Its tabs follow the four-step method.

Right panel — a Chat used for the opening interview, and a Models view showing which AI model does each task (you can ignore this unless you're curious).

How to read each tab

Overview — The whole project at a glance. A checklist of the four steps showing what each one produced (e.g. "411 obligations," "170 operations"), a button to open each finished document, and the Decision Log — every choice a human made, with their name and reason. Start here. If you read nothing else, the Overview tells you where the project stands and lets you open every output.
Purpose — What this project is for, agreed up front. Before any analysis, a short interview pins down: is this a cleanup or a reshape; which rules are in and out of scope; what decision the result is meant to support. This becomes the Purpose Statement, which everything downstream must stay true to. Reading it tells you the project's mandate and boundaries in a sentence or two.
Corpus — The exact set of source rules the project works from. "Corpus" just means "the body of source material." The list is frozen — locked with a fingerprint — so the analysis can't quietly shift onto different sources later. Each entry is a statute, regulation, guidance document, manual, or (as evidence only) an enforcement action.
Derived Blueprint — The rulebook as it actually is, read and organized by AI. This is the first big output. It lists every duty, every defined term, and how the pieces connect — grouped into families. Crucially, it is faithful, not tidy: where the sources contradict each other or define a word two ways, it records both rather than fixing them. Fixing is a later step. Every statement is backed by an exact quote from the source, and a ✓ means the tool checked that the quote really appears there. (How to read the document itself is below.)
Refactor — Proposed clean-ups, one decision at a time. The tool proposes tidy-ups — merge three copies of the same duty, give a term one official definition, retire a dead reference — and you decide each one. Every proposal is labelled with its effect (does it restate the law, clarify it, or actually change it?), and anything that would change the law is set aside rather than done here. This is where human judgment concentrates.
Redesign — Proposed changes that serve stated goals. (Only appears on reshape projects.) Here changing the rules is the point — but under tighter control: first the responsible official adopts a ranked list of objectives (the Mandate), and then every proposed change must name which objective it serves and is signed off by that official. A change with no objective behind it can't even enter the queue.
Target Blueprint — The regime after your decisions. The second big output: the Derived Blueprint plus all the approved changes, each still showing who approved it and why. It also lists what was deliberately left undecided — the honest record of questions parked for later.
Align — Where every original rule ended up. The Crosswalk: for each source provision, was it merged into a consolidated home, repealed, or kept as-is — each with a reason. Nothing vanishes silently. It also runs a check that confirms a "cleanup" really didn't change the law (or, on a reshape, that every change traces to a decision).

How to read the documents it produces

The Derived Blueprint and Target Blueprint documents

Open either with the teal 📄 Open button. Both start with an optional Executive summary in an amber box — a plain-English orientation to the regime and where to focus. The amber box is a reading aid, not the record; if it and the tables ever disagree, the tables win. Below it, the material is grouped by family (a cluster of related rules). Duties appear as table rows — who must do what, when, above what threshold, with what exceptions — each with its source quote. Definitions are listed as given. The Target Blueprint document additionally shows, for each approved change, the operation and the person who approved it.

The Crosswalk document (from Align)

A single table: every original provision on the left, its fate on the right (merged / repealed / kept), and the reason. At the top is the effect-class audit — the line that says, in effect, "we checked, and the cleanup changed nothing it shouldn't have." For a policy reader, that audit line is the trust anchor: it's the machine confirming the human claims.

The Decision Log (on the Overview)

An unchangeable, running list of every decision a person made — ratifying the purpose, disposing of a proposal, freezing the corpus — each stamped with a name, a role, a reason, and a time. This is the project's accountability record. You can hand it to an auditor.

The Defect Register (appendix of the Derived Blueprint)

The flaws the tool found in the source rulebook — duplicated duties, terms defined two ways, dead references, gaps. These are findings, not fixes; the fixes happen in Refactor. Each flaw has a short code (D1–D10) explained in the glossary below.

Glossary — the recurring terms in plain words

Obligation
A duty a rule imposes: who must do what, when.
Definition
A term the rules define — and the tool flags where the same term is defined inconsistently.
Family
A cluster of related rules (e.g. "reporting requirements"), used to organize the blueprint instead of by which agency issued what.
Corpus
The body of source material — the rulebook being analyzed.
Frozen / manifest
The source list is locked with a fingerprint so it can't change unnoticed mid-project.
Defect (D1–D10)
A flaw in the source rulebook the tool found: e.g. D1 conflicting duties, D2 a term defined two ways, D3 duplicates, D4 an undefined term, D5 a dead reference, D9 a gap.
Operation
One proposed change to the blueprint, from a fixed menu (merge, repeal, give-one-definition, etc.). Free-form edits aren't allowed — only named moves.
Effect class
The label on every proposed change: codify (just restates what the law already requires), clarify (resolves an ambiguity), fill-gap (makes explicit a duty that was already implied), change (actually alters what the law requires). In a cleanup, "change" is not allowed — it's set aside.
Disposition
A human's decision on a proposal: accept, modify, reject, or park it.
Mandate / objective (reshape only)
The ranked goals the responsible official adopts; every change in a reshape must serve one.
Misalignment (M1–M5)
A gap between the cleaned rulebook and the goals: e.g. a goal nothing serves, or a rule serving no goal.
Crosswalk
The map of where every old provision went — merged, repealed, or kept.
Provenance / citation
The source trail: every statement links to the exact rule it came from, with the quote checked (✓) against the original.
Ratify
A formal human sign-off that locks an artifact (the purpose, the corpus, the final blueprint).

One line to keep in mind throughout: the tool proposes and checks; people decide. Nothing here is operative law — it's a set of carefully evidenced working papers for the authority that owns the rules.

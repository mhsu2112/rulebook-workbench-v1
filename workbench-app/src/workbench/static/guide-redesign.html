Reviewer's Guide to the Redesign Pass

Companion to the refactor-pass guide — read that one first. This pass runs only in redesign-mode programs (e.g., liquidity), only after the Refactored Blueprint is certified, and only under a Ratified Mandate. Some screens described here arrive with the redesign release; the rules are stable now.

1. How your job changes

In the refactor pass you were a guard. The question on every card was "does this quietly change what the law requires?" — and if it did, your duty was to stop it (park it).

In the redesign pass you are advising a policy process. Changing what the rules require is now the purpose. The discipline doesn't relax — it changes direction. The question on every card becomes: "which adopted objective does this serve, and who authorized wanting it?"

Three things stay exactly the same: every move comes from a fixed whitelist (no free-form edits), every claim carries verified quotes, and every decision is logged with a name and a rationale.

2. The Mandate — the only source of "want"

Before this pass can run, the Principal — the person or body with actual authority over the regime (not the workbench, not the AI, not even the Program Owner acting alone) — has adopted, ranked, and taken ownership of a short list of objectives, stated as outcomes ("depositors can access liquidity within hours of a run starting"), not as instruments ("amend Regulation A"). That list is the Ratified Mandate. It is frozen for the pass.

The cardinal rule of provenance: ideas collected during interviews — including good ones from smart people — are hypotheses, and hypotheses confer no authority. Nothing becomes an objective by default, by silence, or by the AI's enthusiasm. If it isn't in the Ratified Mandate, the workbench cannot want it, and neither can a reviewer.

3. The Misalignment Register (M-codes)

Where the refactor pass worked defects (flaws in the drawing), this pass works misalignments (gaps between the cleaned drawing and the Mandate):

CodePlain English
M1An adopted objective that nothing in the blueprint actually advances.
M2A provision that serves no adopted objective — candidate for repeal, or for a renewed justification.
M3A mechanism that costs far more than its contribution to the objective warrants.
M4Two objectives colliding as implemented — a mechanism advances one by defeating a higher-ranked one.
M5An objective served only by accident of drafting — it works, but fragilely.

M-entries are findings, not decisions — same as D-entries were.

4. What's different on a card

ElementWhat changed from the refactor pass
Effect classchange is now workable — it no longer auto-parks. But it routes to the Principal or named delegate, not to an ordinary Policy Reviewer. Nothing in this pass finalizes automatically — not even codify.
Objective hookNew, mandatory. Every move names the Mandate objective it serves. A move with no hook cannot even enter review — the system refuses it the way it refused fabricated quotes.
RECALIBRATENew move: adjust a threshold, ratio, frequency, deadline, or applicability line. The most consequential move in the toolbox — treat every instance as the policy decision it is.
INTRODUCEUnshackled: it may now create genuinely new duties — under change-class governance, with an objective hook and the Principal's disposition.

5. The tradeoff rule — the line the tool will not cross

A move that advances objective A at a cost to objective B must cite the Mandate's ranking of A over B. If the Mandate doesn't rank them, nobody in the review queue may resolve the conflict — not you, not the AI, not the Program Owner. The question goes back to the Principal as a proposed Mandate amendment. The tool surfaces tradeoffs; it never prices them.

This is the redesign pass's version of the refactor pass's "change must park." When you feel the pull to just decide — that pull is the thing this rule exists for.

6. The Redesign Backlog comes due

Every move parked during the refactor pass ("this would change the law — not our call") lands here, where changing the law is on the table. Each parked item must be dispositioned: adopt (it gets an objective hook and enters the queue), decline (with rationale), or defer (explicitly, with the Principal's logged reasons). The pass cannot close with undispositioned backlog items — the "later" you wrote in the refactor pass arrives now.

7. Worked examples

Example D — M2: a provision serving no objective

Card: REPEAL change "Retire the weekly paper attestation requirement; no Mandate objective is served by it." Hook: none claimed — cites M2 finding.

Step 1 — check the M2 finding. Is it true that no objective is served? Look for indirect service (M5): does the attestation feed anything an objective needs?

Step 2 — remember what M2 permits. An M2 finding supports either repeal or renewed justification. If the Principal values the provision, the honest path is amending the Mandate to say so — not quietly keeping it.

Step 3 — route. REPEAL of an operative duty is change-class → Principal disposition. Your role as reviewer: verify the finding is sound and the record complete, then pass it up with your note.

Example E — RECALIBRATE with a clean hook

Card: RECALIBRATE change "Shift liquidity-report cadence for run-susceptible institutions from monthly to daily-on-trigger." Hook: Objective 2 — supervisors detect run dynamics within hours.

Step 1 — test the hook. Does the move actually advance the cited objective, or just gesture at it? Here: cadence is precisely the mechanism the objective needs — hook is real.

Step 2 — look for the losing objective. Faster reporting costs burden (Objective 5, say: proportionate compliance costs). Does the Mandate rank 2 over 5? If yes, the move cites that ranking and proceeds to the Principal. If the Mandate is silent — stop; tradeoff rule; back to the Principal as an amendment question.

Step 3 — check the evidence. Quotes verified? Does the burden claim rest on operative sources or on advocacy material? (Reform proposals inform design; they still don't establish current-law claims.)

Example F — the redesign trap: enthusiasm without authority

Card: INTRODUCE change "Require pre-positioning of collateral at the discount window" — an idea a respected interviewee proposed, and the AI drafted persuasively. Hook cited: "Mandate Hypothesis H-3."

Step 1 — read the hook carefully. H-3 is a hypothesis — collected in an interview, never adopted by the Principal. A hypothesis is not an objective.

Step 2 — apply the provenance rule. However good the idea, it has no authority behind it. The move cannot enter review with this hook. It is not rejected on the merits — it is returned: the Principal may adopt H-3 into the Mandate (logged, attributed), after which the move comes back with a real hook.

Step 3 — note the asymmetry. In the refactor pass the trap was a change dressed as a clarification. Here the trap is authority dressed as analysis — the AI's fluency making an unadopted idea feel decided. Same discipline, new costume.

8. Golden rules (redesign edition)

① No hook, no review — an unhooked move isn't rejected, it's returned. ② Unranked tradeoffs go up, never sideways — the Principal prices; you surface. ③ The Mandate is frozen during the pass; wanting to edit it mid-review is a sign the pass found something real — log it for the amendment step, don't improvise. ④ Hypotheses confer no authority, no matter how good they sound — especially then. ⑤ The Backlog must end empty of undecided items: adopt, decline, or defer-with-reasons. ⑥ Every objective must end served or explicitly deferred — orphan objectives fail the gate too.

Exit: the Principal has dispositioned every change-class move, every Mandate objective is served or deferred with reasons, the Backlog is empty of undecided items, and the Program Owner ratifies the Target Blueprint. What the working group then holds is not "what the AI thinks the rules should be" — it is the Principal's adopted objectives, made structural, with the receipts.

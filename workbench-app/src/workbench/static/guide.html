Reviewer's Guide to the Refactor Queue

Plain-English instructions for dispositioning operation cards. No technical background needed. Ten minutes to read; keep it open in a tab while you work.

1. What you are actually doing

The workbench read every rule in the corpus, built a "blueprint" of what the regime requires, and found defects — duplicated duties, conflicting definitions, dead references. For each defect, an AI has proposed a tidy-up move. Your job is to decide, move by move, whether each proposal is truly just a tidy-up — or whether it would quietly change what the law requires.

Three things to hold onto:

Nothing happens without you. A proposal is only a proposal until a human reviewer dispositions it. Everything you decide is logged — your name, your reasoning, the time — in an append-only record. And you cannot break anything: the system refuses forbidden moves (it will tell you why), and every underlying document is preserved untouched.

2. Reading a card, top to bottom

What you seeWhat it means
MERGE etc.The type of move proposed — see the table in §3.
draft: clarify (medium)The AI's guess at the move's legal effect, with its confidence. This is advice, not a decision. A second AI (a different brand, on purpose) wrote this guess; you make the actual call in the form below.
The proposal sentenceWhat the move would do to the blueprint — e.g., "merge these three statements of the same duty into one."
targets / quotes n/n verifiedWhich documents the move touches, and whether the AI's supporting quotes were found word-for-word in the sources by an automatic check. ✓ means the quote is real. A card full of ✗ should not be trusted.
▸ detailsOpens the AI's full reasoning and its quotes. Read this before deciding — especially the "draft classification" section, which explains why it guessed the effect class it did.
The formYour decision: an effect class, an action, and a one-or-two-sentence rationale. §4 and §5.

3. The moves, in plain English

MovePlain English
MERGESeveral provisions say the same thing — fold them into one.
SPLITOne provision does two unrelated jobs — separate them.
REPEALRetire a provision (e.g., long superseded but never removed).
RELOCATEMove a provision to where readers would look for it.
CANONICALIZE-DEFINITIONOne term, several definitions — pick one official definition.
DEFINE-TERMAn important term is used but never defined — give it a definition.
SUBSTITUTE-TERMSwap a term for a consistent one — meaning must not change.
NORMALIZE-ELEMENTSRestate a duty in the standard who / must / what / when / threshold / exceptions form.
FACTOR-EXCEPTIONPull a buried exception out into an explicit, labeled exception.
ELEVATE-GENERAL-RULEThe same rule is repeated in many places — state it once, generally.
RESOLVE-CROSS-REFERENCEFix a reference that points at nothing or the wrong place.
RELATE-OBLIGATIONRecord that two duties are connected (one specializes the other, etc.).
INTRODUCEAdd something new to the blueprint. Handle with care — allowed here only if the duty already exists in the sources and the move merely makes it visible. See Example B.

4. Effect class — the one question that matters

Every move gets an effect class. It answers: "compared to what the law already requires, what does this move do?" Your dropdown selection — not the AI's badge — is the official classification.

ClassPlain EnglishWhat the system does with it
codifyThe law already requires exactly this. The move just writes it down cleanly.May be finalized.
clarifyThe law supports this, but ambiguously — the move picks the sensible reading.Needs your individual decision.
fill_gapThe duty already exists in the sources, but the blueprint only implied it — the move makes it explicit. Your rationale must say which source establishes the duty.Needs your individual decision.
changeThis would alter what the law requires — add, remove, or shift a duty. Cannot be finalized here. It parks to the Redesign Backlog. The system enforces this; if you pick "change" + "accept" it will refuse.
unresolvedYou genuinely can't tell what the baseline requires.Treated like change — parks. This is the honest choice when unsure.
The trap to watch for: a move that looks like a small clarification but is actually a change. Two tells: (1) the only support is an enforcement action or a proposed rule — neither can establish a duty on its own; (2) the "clarification" would resolve a genuine conflict between two live sources — picking a winner is a policy choice, not a tidy-up. In both cases: classify change, park it.

5. The four actions

ActionUse it when…
acceptThe move is right as proposed and its class permits finalizing.
modifyThe move is right in spirit but needs rewording — type your corrected version; yours becomes the record.
rejectThe move is wrong: misreads the source, unnecessary, or the quotes don't support it.
parkThe move may have merit but changes the law (or you can't tell) — it goes to the Redesign Backlog for the policy phase. Parking is a good outcome, not a failure — a well-stocked backlog is the honest record that this corpus can't be fully fixed without policy decisions.

The rationale box: one or two honest sentences a colleague could audit later. "Duplicate wording, no substantive difference between the three sources" beats a paragraph.

6. Worked examples

Example A — the straightforward accept

Card: CANONICALIZE-DEFINITION draft: clarify (high) "Adopt the FinCEN regulation's definition of monetary instruments as canonical; the FFIEC manual's paraphrase differs in wording only." Quotes 3/3 ✓.

Step 1 — open details. Read both quoted definitions side by side. Ask: do they differ in substance (would anything be covered by one but not the other?) or only in wording?

Step 2 — check the hierarchy. The regulation is binding law; the exam manual is guidance. Making the higher-authority text canonical doesn't change anyone's obligations.

Step 3 — classify. Wording-only divergence, resolved toward the binding source: clarify is right. Keep it.

Step 4 — act. Action accept. Rationale: "Definitions differ in wording only; canonical text is the binding regulation's. No coverage difference."

Example B — the trap (looks small, is a change)

Card: INTRODUCE draft: fill_gap (medium) "Make explicit an obligation to update the risk assessment after every product launch — implied by the corpus." Quotes 2/2 ✓, but the details show both quotes come from enforcement action summaries.

Step 1 — check what kind of source the quotes come from. Both are enforcement documents. The rule of the house: enforcement actions show what examiners expected — they cannot, on their own, establish what the law requires.

Step 2 — ask the fill_gap question. Does any binding source establish this duty? Search the details; here, none is cited.

Step 3 — classify honestly. Without a binding anchor, writing this duty into the blueprint would create it. That is change — even though the AI drafted "fill_gap" and the quotes are genuine.

Step 4 — act. Action park. Rationale: "Only support is enforcement evidence; no operative source establishes the duty. New requirement → policy decision, parked for redesign." The idea isn't lost — it waits in the backlog for the phase where new duties may lawfully be considered.

Example C — the reject

Card: MERGE draft: codify (medium) "Merge the bank and MSB independent-testing obligations — same duty stated twice." Quotes 2/2 ✓.

Step 1 — compare the element tables. The quotes are real, but the details show the two obligations differ: one requires testing by a qualified outside party or untasked personnel; the other permits self-testing at a different frequency. Different actors, different thresholds.

Step 2 — decide. This is not one duty stated twice; it's two duties that rhyme. Merging them would erase a real difference — a substantive error, not a class question.

Step 3 — act. Action reject. Rationale: "Not duplicates: testing personnel and frequency requirements differ between banks and MSBs. Merge would erase a real distinction." (If you thought the two should be aligned — that's a policy view; the place for it is the redesign phase, not this card.)

7. Golden rules

① When unsure, unresolved — it parks safely and can be revisited; a wrong "accept" is the only mistake that costs anything. ② Your class choice beats the AI's badge — that's the design, not an override. ③ Never resolve a genuine conflict between two live sources at this stage — that's a policy choice wearing a tidy-up's clothes. ④ Quotes verified ✓ means the words are real, not that the argument is right — Example C. ⑤ One honest sentence of rationale; your future self is the audience.

The final step — ratifying the Target Blueprint — belongs to the Program Owner and locks the record. If you're not the Program Owner, your work ends when the review queue is empty.
